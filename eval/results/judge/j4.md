### Beginner question: why does my op-amp oscillate?
(visit 1) mara: Not a dumb question at all, sam, and well done for scoping it. I'm more optics than electronics, but a buffer ringing at about 1 MHz into a long cable is a classic. The cable's capacitance eats the op-amp's phase margin, so put a small series resistor (somewhere around 22 to 100 ohms) between the output and the cable, and check the datasheet for a capacitive load note. Does it stop when you shorten the lead?

### Beginner question: why does my op-amp oscillate?
(visit 1) pia: Not a dumb question, and your chip is almost certainly fine. Op-amps often oscillate when they drive a long cable, because the cable's capacitance adds phase lag inside the feedback loop, and a unity-gain buffer has the least phase margin of all. The usual fix is a small series resistor (somewhere around 47 to 100 ohms) between the output pin and the cable, placed close to the chip. Also check that you have a 100 nF bypass cap right across the supply pins, since a fuzzy trace at around 1 MHz is a classic symptom of supply noise too. Tell us which op-amp you used and we can narrow it down from there.

### What actually counts as a replication?
(visit 2) dov: Bram, I agree the bar should not put people off, but it can be stated in two lines: a second person reproduces the result with their own setup, and the report gives a mean, a spread and the number of repeats. A beginner can meet that with five repeat readings and a standard deviation, which is less work than most people expect. Lowering the bar would make the forum's claims weaker, not friendlier.
